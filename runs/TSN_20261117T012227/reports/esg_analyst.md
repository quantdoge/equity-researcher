## 2. Environmental Risk Assessment: **HIGH**

**Positives (partial credit only).** Tyson discloses Scope 3 including agriculture and land-use change — better than many food peers — and has a Scope 3-inclusive target of **–30% GHG by 2030** and **net zero by 2050**. Ceres' Food Emissions 50 assessment credits disclosure and target-setting.

**Why High, not Medium:**
- **No SBTi-validated 1.5°C pathway.** Ceres marks Tyson short of a validated, 1.5°C-aligned Scope 3 target. RLAM's 2026 Climate Transition Assessment found emissions targets but **"no credible transition plan"** — no interim milestones, no board-level accountability. This is a target without a capital plan.
- **Deforestation commitments have slipped repeatedly.** Forest-protection commitments now state deforestation-free **beef not until 2028** and **soy/corn not until 2030**, after an earlier 2025 deadline was missed. This is a pattern of rolling deadlines — the single most predictive indicator of greenwashing-adjacent disclosure risk.
- **Emissions are structurally Scope 3-heavy and beef-weighted.** Beef is the highest-carbon protein in the portfolio, and beef is also where Tyson is currently losing money (Q4 FY26 operating loss **$171M**, volumes **–12.7%**). Retrenchment during a beef cyclical trough historically deprioritises transition capex — a classic ESG-cyclicality trap.
- **Nature/biodiversity:** no articulated biodiversity strategy or soil-health commitments (Nature Action 100 engagement since 2019 with limited progress).
- **Physical risk is already in the P&L.** Tightest cattle supply in decades, Plains drought exposure, feed cost volatility. Climate risk here is not a 2050 story; it is a 2026 margin story.

**Regulatory severity risk:** EU Deforestation Regulation exposure with penalties of **up to 4% of EU turnover** for non-compliance — the largest single hard-number environmental liability identified.

## 3. Social Risk Assessment: **CRITICAL**

This is Tyson's defining ESG exposure, and it is asymmetric — tail risk with unlimited downside per incident.

- **Child labour in the supply chain.** Following the 2023 DOL inquiries into migrant children in Tyson and Perdue slaughterhouses, allegations have recurred (2024, 2025 whistleblower claims in Arkansas), triggering **Senate HELP Committee investigations (Sens. Cassidy and Hawley)** and a formal investor request for an independent third-party audit of labour practices (2024 proposal). RLAM's March 2026 note explicitly cites **"recent litigation relating to allegations of child labour"** as an open concern. Industry-wide contractor sanitation exposure (PSSI) is a systemic channel Tyson has not ring-fenced with verified disclosure.
- **Worker safety.** OSHA severe-injury data analysed by EPI found Tyson, with 33% more workers than Ford, reported **six times the number of severe injuries**. Persistent reporting (including allegations of under-recording via in-house clinics) suggests both physical and *disclosure* risk.
- **Immigration.** A 2026 shareholder proposal specifically requested **immigration-related risk disclosure**; the board recommended rejection and it failed. In a 133,000-person, immigrant-heavy workforce under active enforcement, non-disclosure is itself the risk.
- **Responsiveness deficit.** A 2021 human-rights due diligence proposal drew **78.7% independent shareholder support** yet produced no binding policy change — evidence that social risk is not being governed, only managed communicatively.
- **Sanitation/gig labour:** contract labour across plants remains the weakest link in the human-rights chain.

**Financial channel:** DOL hot-goods detention, retailer sourcing audits, and customer ESG mandates (McDonald's, Walmart, KFC) can each interrupt revenue with little warning. Prepared Foods — now **over half of operating profit at 12.6% margins** — depends on exactly these customer relationships.

## 4. Governance Quality Score: **3 / 10** (Weak)

| Item | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Dual-class control** | Class B carries **10 votes/share vs 1 for Class A**, controlled via **Tyson Limited Partnership**. Founding family holds voting control materially disproportionate to economic ownership. Class B is not publicly traded. **Severe.** |
| **Board independence** | 15 directors; chairman **John H. Tyson** (family, former CEO) — chair not independent. RLAM **voted against 6 directors in 2025 and escalated to 7 in 2026** over independence, climate oversight and the multi-class structure. |
| **Board responsiveness** | Three shareholder proposals defeated at the 5 Feb 2026 AGM (immigration risk disclosure; voting-results-by-class transparency; third on oversight). Board recommended rejection of all three. 78.7% prior support ignored. **Weak.** |
| **CEO succession** | **Jeff Schomburger**, a **10-year board member**, named CEO effective 4 Oct 2026, succeeding Donnie King (43-year veteran, remains on board). Board-to-CEO moves entangle oversight with management and foreclose an external search — a recognised governance negative. |
| **Compensation alignment** | The Annual Incentive Plan's performance-measure menu is **entirely financial** — EPS, operating cash flow, net income, TSR, ROIC, ROE, EBIT/EBITDA (13 measures). **Zero weighting** to safety, labour, climate or human rights. Incentives do not pay for ESG remediation. |
| **Antitrust/conduct governance** | Serial price-fixing settlements (see §5) indicate a compliance culture problem that has persisted across management eras. |
| **Positives** | Separate Chair/CEO roles; all three AGM proposals permitted on ballot; Scope 3 disclosure quality above peer median. |

Score justified at 3, not 1–2, because the company does disclose, does allow proposals, and the board was partially refreshed — but control is structurally entrenched and accountability mechanisms are demonstrably non-functional.

## 6. Regulatory ESG Exposure

| Jurisdiction / rule | Exposure |
|---|---|
| **EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR)** | **High.** Cattle and soy are in scope; penalties **up to 4% of EU turnover**. Tyson serves EU/UK foodservice and retail via its European operations. Hard, quantified, near-term |
| **EU CSRD / ESRS** | **Medium-High.** A large EU subsidiary footprint plus non-EU group thresholds pulls Tyson into CSRD reporting; requires value-chain (Scope 3) and human-rights due diligence at a granularity Tyson has not yet demonstrated |
| **California SB 253 / SB 261** | **High.** Tyson clearly exceeds the $1B doing-business threshold. Initial **Scope 1 & 2 reporting due 10 Aug 2026**, Scope 3 phased from 2027; climate risk report under SB 261. Tyson's Scope 3 methodology exists but lacks assurance |
| **US DOL / FLSA child labour** | **Critical tail risk.** "Hot goods" provisions permit detention of goods produced with illegal labour — a direct revenue-interruption mechanism; plus Senate oversight |
| **US SEC climate rule** | **Low (currently).** Stays in litigation; not a binding near-term driver |
| **US state anti-ESG pushback (TX, AR, FL)** | **Offsetting.** Reduces investor-driven disclosure pressure and lowers litigation risk on ESG *statements*; does not reduce operational/retailer-driven risk |
| **ESG-linked debt covenants** | Not identified in retrieved material — likely none material |

## 7. Overall ESG Rating: **LAGGARD**

Tyson sits in the bottom quartile of large-cap consumer staples on governance and social dimensions. It has a **disclosure apparatus without an accountability apparatus**: credible Scope 3 measurement, partial targets, and a published sustainability report — sitting on top of a dual-class-controlled board that has defeated or ignored repeated investor requests (78.7% support on human rights; 6–7 directors opposed by a major UK manager; three proposals rejected in 2026), a compensation plan with no ESG metrics, rolling deforestation deadlines that have already slipped 3–6 years, and the most serious unresolved labour/human-rights allegations in US food manufacturing.

The most important insight for valuation: **Tyson's ESG risk is concentrated in tail events that are legally enabled rather than gradually priced** — a DOL hot-goods action, a major retailer delisting over child labour, or an EUDR penalty at 4% of EU turnover. These are low-probability/high-severity, which means they show up as *cost of capital and event risk* rather than in forward earnings — exactly the kind of risk that standard screens miss.

## 8. ESG Investment Implication: **NEGATIVE (as a risk overlay) — NET NEUTRAL to the base case**

**For the thesis, ESG is a headwind on quality of earnings, not a driver of the numbers.**

- **Not a valuation catalyst.** The stock's actual problem is cyclical and operational: beef losses, cattle supply, FY27 comparable sales guided **–3.5% to –5.5%**. ESG does not change that arithmetic. Shares at ~$63.95 after a rebound from ~$50 (Sept 2026) already reflect the operational recovery story.
- **But it argues against paying for quality.** A 3/10 governance score, a $22.5B market cap with family voting control, and ~$230M of recurring antitrust settlements justify a **discount to consumer-staples peers on governance grounds**, not a premium. Justified use of the ~0.375 beta's defensive appeal is as a bond-proxy, not a compounder.
- **Asymmetric exposure.** Prepared Foods (>50% of operating profit, 12.6% margins) is the value driver and is *directly* exposed to the social risk map — customer ESG mandates and labour-standards audits. That is where an incident would hurt most.
- **Stewardship action.** Vote against the chair, the compensation committee, and directors flagged for independence (following RLAM's 7-director position). Support the voting-results-by-class, immigration-risk, and independent labour-audit resolutions. Escalate via Nature Action 100 and Ceres. The realistic ask is not net-zero acceleration — it is **a human-rights audit with teeth and an ESG weight in the annual incentive plan**, both cheap and both currently absent.

Conclusions on governance are nonetheless corroborated by two independent sources (RLAM voting record; AGM proposal outcomes).
