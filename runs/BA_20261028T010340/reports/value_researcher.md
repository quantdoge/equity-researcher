# The Boeing Company (BA) — Value Investment Analysis
**Price: $168.18 (Oct 27, 2026 close) | Shares: 781M | Market cap: $131.4B | Net debt: $43.5B | EV: ~$175B**

---

## 1. Business Quality Assessment — Moat Rating: **NARROW (trending narrower)**

**The structural case.** Boeing and Airbus constitute one of the great duopolies in global industry. Barriers are formidable and real: type certification by the FAA/EASA (a decade and $10-20B to bring a clean-sheet narrowbody to market), 30-40 year airframe lives that lock airlines into a spare-parts and maintenance ecosystem, pilot type-rating switching costs, and a supplier base so concentrated that Boeing *is* the market for its tiers. On paper this is a Wide moat.

**The evidence says otherwise.** A moat is only worth what management extracts from it, and the last six years show Boeing converting a Wide moat into a Narrow one:

- **$36B of cumulative net losses** from 2019-2024, and LTM operating income of **–$150M on $94.9B of revenue** — an operating margin of roughly 0.0%. In 2018, on $101B of revenue, Boeing earned a 10.0% operating margin. Revenue has recovered to ~94% of peak; **margins have recovered to zero.** That gap is the entire investment question.
- **Share loss to Airbus.** Airbus has held the majority of narrowbody orders for years while 737 MAX production was capped post-grounding and the 777X slipped to ~2027.
- **Pricing power is impaired**, not intact. The company is absorbing billions in fixed-price defense development charges (KC-46, VC-25B, T-7A) and paying for its own quality failures. A business with true pricing power does not do this for six consecutive years.
- **ROIC is below WACC and has been for four years.** FY2025's reported 7.15% ROIC is an artefact of a one-off non-operating gain; 2022-2024 ROIC was –8.5%, –2.3%, –21.5%.

**Verdict:** The moat exists but is currently *impaired in Boeing's hands*. I rate it Narrow. If Ortberg restores factory stability and the 737/787 rate ramps hold, this could be re-rated Wide — but that is the thesis, not the fact.

## 2. Financial Quality Score: **3 / 10**

| Metric | LTM (Q4'25–Q3'26) | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $94.9B | Recovered — genuinely good |
| Operating income | **–$150M** | ~0% margin vs 10% at 2018 peak |
| Net income (adjusted) | **–$564M** | Reported +$7.7B is a one-off |
| Free cash flow | **–$1.54B** | 5 of last 6 years FCF-negative |
| Total debt / cash | $54.4B / $29.4B | Net debt $43.5B |
| Shareholders' equity | **$5.5B** | On $168B of assets |
| Interest expense | ~$2.45B/yr | ~16x LTM operating income |
| Inventory | **$84.7B** | 0.89x revenue vs ~0.5x historically |
| Diluted shares | 568M (2020) → 781M (2026) | **+37% dilution** |

Two things stand out as disqualifying for a Buffett-style quality screen:

1. **FCF conversion is broken.** Owner earnings (NI + D&A – maintenance capex) were +$1.25B in FY2025 and negative in each of the three prior years. The core issue is working capital: $84.7B of inventory — roughly 326 days of revenue — sits on the balance sheet in partially-built airframes. Every rate increase *consumes* cash before it produces it. Boeing must fund the recovery before it can harvest it.

2. **The balance sheet has no cushion.** $5.5B of equity supporting $54B of debt is a ~10:1 leverage ratio. The company has already raised equity twice (2020, 2024) at deeply disadvantageous prices to survive. A third raise in a recession or another grounding is a live possibility, and it would be dilutive at $168 — a price I already consider too high.

**The one genuine asset:** a backlog on the order of $600B. That is real, contracted, and in most scenarios will be delivered. It is what makes this a *troubled good business* rather than a bad one. But backlog is not cash flow, and Boeing has spent seven years proving the two are not the same thing.

## 3. Valuation Analysis — Intrinsic Value ≈ **$100/share (range $80–$125)**

The reported multiples are uninformative (67.8x P/E, 23.5x P/B, negative P/FCF). P/E on a company earning approximately nothing is noise. So I value the business on two independent normalized approaches.

**Method A — Normalized DCF (success case).**
Assume by 2030-31: revenue $110B, operating margin 9% (below the 2018 peak of 10.0%, reflecting post-strike wage structure and supplier cost inflation) → EBIT $9.9B; interest $2.5B; tax 21% → net income ~$5.85B; normalized FCF ~$6.0B. Ramp FCF 2027-31 at $0.5B / $2.5B / $4.5B / $5.5B / $6.0B, WACC 8.5%, terminal growth 2.5%.

- PV of explicit FCFs: $14.1B
- PV of terminal value: $68.2B
- EV: $82.3B → less net debt $43.5B → **equity $38.8B → ~$50/share**

**Method B — 2030 exit multiple.**
2030 revenue $105B at a 12% EBITDA margin = $12.6B EBITDA. At 12x EV/EBITDA (a mature, wide-moat aerospace multiple) = $151B EV; assume net debt paid down to $25B → $126B equity / 790M shares = **$160/share in 2030**, discounted at 8.5% for four years = **~$115/share today.**

**Blended intrinsic value: ~$100/share**, with the honest range being $50 (DCF) to $125 (exit multiple). Note that Method B, the *more generous* method, embeds an assumption that Boeing both hits peak-cycle margins on record revenue AND repays $18B of net debt — while also being the more common way the sell-side gets to its $225-305 targets.

**What the current price requires.** At $168.18 you are paying ~$175B of enterprise value for a company with negative LTM FCF. To justify that on a 15x EV/EBITDA exit in 2030, Boeing needs ~$11.7B of EBITDA — i.e., **full normalization, no discount, no dilution, no further charges.** The market is paying today for a recovery that has not yet appeared in a single clean quarter.

**Margin of safety test:** To buy at a 25% discount to a $100 intrinsic value I would need $75. The stock trades at a **~68% premium** to my estimate. There is not merely no margin of safety here; the sign is wrong.

*Caveat on multiples:* peer medians (GE Aerospace $309B, RTX $263B, Lockheed $144B market cap) put the sector median P/E near 36.7x on far higher-quality earnings. Boeing's 67.8x is a 85% premium to peers for demonstrably inferior economics — the opposite of what a value investor wants to pay.

## 4. Management Quality Assessment — **Mixed, improving but unproven**

**The positive:** CEO Kelly Ortberg (since Aug 2024) is the first Boeing chief in two decades with genuine operator credibility — he ran Rockwell Collins and understands factory floors, not just financial engineering. His agenda is correct: settle the machinist strike, stabilize 737/787 rate before pushing it, cut overhead, and shrink to core. The 777X is finally near certification and the F-47 NGAD win gives BDS a decade-long franchise.

**The negative — capital allocation history is among the worst in large-cap industrials.** From 2013-2019 Boeing spent roughly **$43B on buybacks at $150-440**, retiring ~20% of shares at peak prices. It then raised over $30B of debt and equity at the bottom to survive. That is a textbook value-destruction cycle: buy high, sell low. The dividend — once $8.22/share annually — was suspended in 2020 and remains a token ~$0.45/share (0.27% yield). Insider ownership is negligible; this is a management of stewards, not owners.

**Scorecard:** Right strategy, right CEO, but zero track record of execution under pressure and a governance and incentive structure that has repeatedly rewarded short-term optics over long-term cash generation. I would not pay a quality premium for this management team. They must earn it first.

---

## 5. Investment Verdict: **SELL / AVOID**

I cannot construct a scenario that justifies $168 with a margin of safety. The business generates no free cash, earns a return below its cost of capital, carries $43.5B of net debt against $5.5B of equity, has diluted shareholders 37% in six years, and trades at a substantial premium to a generously-constructed intrinsic value.

**This is not a bad company.** It is a strategically essential, duopoly-protected franchise with a $600B backlog that will very likely be worth considerably more in 2032 than it is today. But "will eventually recover" and "is cheap today" are different propositions. Buffett's discipline is to wait for the price to reflect the risk. At $100-110 I would begin to be interested. At $75 I would be a buyer. At $168 I am a seller.

Anyone buying here is underwriting an execution turnaround — a bet on operational certainty in a business that has delivered operational surprises in eight consecutive years. That is a venture-style bet dressed as a value investment.

## 6. Key Risks to the Value Thesis (i.e., reasons I could be wrong)

1. **Faster-than-modeled normalization.** If 737 rate hits 42/month and 787 hits 10/month with clean quality, FCF could inflect to +$4-6B far sooner than 2029, and the ramp in my Method A is too conservative. **This is the single largest risk to my Sell.**
2. **Working capital release is optionality I've underweighted.** Delivering down $84.7B of inventory releases cash at a rate that could dwarf earnings for several quarters. A $10B inventory release alone is ~$13/share.
3. **Deleveraging via divestiture.** Non-core assets (Jeppesen, Digital, ULA stake, perhaps BDS units) could retire $10-15B of debt, materially lifting equity value in Method A.
4. **Sell-side consensus is uniformly bullish** ($225-305 targets, most at $250-278). The Street is capitalizing 2029 earnings. If their timeline is right and mine is wrong by two years, they win.
5. **Sovereign/strategic value.** Boeing is a US national-security asset; a distressed price invites political support, and the F-47 and NASA franchises anchor defense revenue.
6. **Data-availability caveat:** my intrinsic value hinges on a normalized margin assumption (9%) that cannot be directly observed in the reported data; segment-level profitability was unavailable, so I could not isolate Global Services' cash generation from Commercial Airplanes' losses. If BGS is materially more profitable than assumed, the trough is shallower than it appears.

**Downside risks to the stock if I'm right:** another quality grounding, a failed 777X certification milestone, a recession-driven order deferral cycle, or a third equity raise would each plausibly take the stock to $90-110 — which is roughly where intrinsic value sits. That asymmetry (limited upside to fair value, substantial downside to a re-rating on bad news) is the core of the Sell.

---

**Bottom line: Narrow moat, 3/10 financial quality, ~$100 intrinsic value vs. $168 price. SELL.** Revisit below $110; build a position below $75.
