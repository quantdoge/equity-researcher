# Sysco Corporation (NYSE: SYY) — Value Investment Analysis
**Price: $76.94 | Market cap ~$37.0B | EV ~$50.2B | Fiscal year ends late June**

## 1. Business Quality Assessment — Moat: **NARROW** (trend: stable/eroding at the edges)

Sysco is the largest North American broadline foodservice distributor: $84.6B revenue, ~17% U.S. share, delivering to roughly 400,000 restaurant, healthcare, education and hospitality locations.

**Sources of moat:**
- **Scale economies in route density** — the genuine moat. Broadline distribution is a fixed-cost network; profitability is driven by drops per mile. Sysco's local density is unmatched, and its purchasing scale supports private-label penetration (Sysco Brand) that carries higher margins.
- **Intangible assets / incumbency** — 50+ years of relationships, and delivery is operationally embedded in customers' kitchens (ordering systems, credit, next-day reliability). This is real but **weak** switching cost — a chef can switch distributors in a week.
- **Efficient scale** — new entrants cannot replicate a national refrigerated network; but this is a duopoly-not-quite: US Foods, Performance Food Group, Costco Business Center, and cash-and-carry all compete aggressively.

**Honest evidence the moat is not wide:**
- Gross margin has been **flat at 18.3–18.5% for six consecutive years** (17.95% → 18.50%). A business with pricing power expands gross margin. Sysco has none — it passes through inflation and buys share with price.
- Operating margin is thin and **compressing**: 4.06% (FY24) → 3.80% (FY25) → 3.66% (FY26).
- **ROIC has fallen every year: 23.0% → 21.6% → 18.9% → 17.6%.** Still comfortably above WACC (~6.5–7.5%), so value is being created — but the direction is wrong.
- Customers are the weakest link in the food chain: independent restaurants with high failure rates, extreme price sensitivity, and no loyalty.

**Verdict:** a good, scale-protected business — not a great one. It is a *toll collector on other people's calories*, earning ~17% returns on capital in a 3.7%-margin industry. Narrow moat, and I would not pay a wide-moat price for it.

## 2. Financial Quality Score: **6 / 10**

| Operating margin | 4.0% | 4.1% | 3.8% | 3.7% |

| FCF ($B) | 2.08 | 2.16 | 1.60 | 1.94 |
| Owner earnings ($B) | 1.87 | 2.12 | 2.01 | 2.19 |
| Net debt ($B) | 10.4 | 12.2 | 13.4 | 13.2 |
| ROIC | 23.0% | 21.6% | 18.9% | 17.6% |

**Strengths:** FCF conversion is excellent — FY26 FCF/net income = **110%**. Owner earnings have grown from $1.87B to $2.19B in three years. Revenue is highly predictable (+3.9%, +3.2%, +3.3% the last three years; the FY22–23 spikes were inflation pass-through, not real growth). Working capital is modestly negative-consuming, and the model is genuinely recession-resistant (people eat out less but Sysco's customers still buy food).

**Weaknesses (why not an 8+):**
1. **GAAP EPS is declining** — $3.89 → $3.73 → $3.66 — despite 7% cumulative revenue growth. Management reports **adjusted FY26 EPS of ~$4.61**, a ~26% gap driven by "transformational project costs," acquisition costs and restructuring. These have recurred for years and are real cash outlays (~$210M in Q1 FY27 alone). I will not capitalize adjusted earnings.
2. **Leverage is high**: net debt/EBITDA **3.2x**, interest coverage 4.2x, and interest expense rose from $527M (FY23) to $717M (FY26). Total equity is only $2.67B against $14.97B of debt — an artifact of buybacks, but it leaves almost no balance-sheet cushion.
3. **FY26 capex of $700M was well below D&A of $1,129M** (FY23–25: $793M–$906M). Normalizing capex upward to ~$1.0B cuts true FCF to roughly **$1.65–1.70B**, not $1.94B. That materially changes the valuation math below.
4. Thin margins mean small shocks (fuel costs, wage investment, food-cost deflation) swing earnings hard — Q1 FY27 operating income fell 10.4% GAAP.

## 3. Valuation Analysis

**Current multiples (at $76.94, GAAP basis):** P/E 21.0x | P/adj-EPS ~16.7x | EV/EBITDA 12.2x | P/FCF 19.1x (21.7x on normalized capex) | **FCF yield 4.6–5.2%** | Owner-earnings yield 5.9% | Dividend yield 2.86%

**Standalone intrinsic value.** Using normalized owner earnings of ~$2.0B, 4–5% growth for five years tapering to 3.5%, 2.5% terminal growth, and a 7.5–8.0% discount rate (beta 0.64 flatters the cost of equity; I use a premium for thin margins and pending leverage):

- At 7.5% WACC: EV ≈ $47B → equity ≈ $34B → **~$71/share**
- At 8.0% WACC: EV ≈ $43B → equity ≈ $30B → **~$62/share**

Cross-check on normalized earnings power: ~$4.30/share of sustainable GAAP-equivalent EPS (haircutting roughly half the recurring "one-time" add-backs) at 17–18x = **$73–77**. Both methods converge on **fair value of $68–78, midpoint ~$72**.

**The current price embeds the deal, not the standalone business.** At $76.94 the market is paying full standalone value *plus* option value on Jetro. The $76.94 price equals roughly 100–105% of my standalone intrinsic value — I require a 20–30% discount. **There is no margin of safety.**

**The Jetro Restaurant Depot transaction (announced 3/30/26 — the reason the stock fell 15% that day):**

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Consideration | $21.6B cash + 91.5M SYY shares (19% dilution) |
| Total EV | **$29.1B** = 14.6x EBIT, ~13.9x EBITDA |
| Jetro CY25 | ~$16B revenue, ~$2.1B EBITDA, **~$1.9B FCF**, 166 stores, 725k customers |
| Pro forma net debt | ~$34.8B (13.2 + 21.6) |
| Pro forma EBITDA | ~$6.2B → **~5.6x levered** |
| Pro forma FCF/share | ~$5.20 vs ~$4.03 standalone (**+29%**) |

Note what this deal reveals: **Jetro earns a ~12% FCF margin on $16B of revenue, versus Sysco's 2.3%.** Management is buying a *better* business than its own — an implicit admission about broadline distribution's economics. It is also buying it at 14.6x EBIT with borrowed money, which is roughly a fair price for a wholesaler, not a bargain. If it closes and synergies land, the combined entity at $76.94 trades at ~14.7x pro forma FCF and would be genuinely attractive. If it is blocked, shareholders keep a standalone business worth ~$72 and recoup the deal costs. I do not underwrite transformational, heavily-levered, antitrust-exposed M&A as a source of margin of safety.

**Street context:** analyst targets $84–$91 (Morgan Stanley $91, Guggenheim $90, Piper $84), a median implied upside of ~15%. Deutsche Bank downgraded to Hold in April. Consensus is built on adjusted EPS; my analysis is not.

## 4. Management & Capital Allocation

**Mixed, tilting to concerning.**
- CEO Kevin Hourican (since 2020) and CFO Brandon Sewell have executed well operationally on local volume growth (+2.7% in Q1 FY27, a genuine win in a soft restaurant-traffic environment) and are pursuing AI-driven cost savings ($100M in FY27, ~2% of operating expense).
- Historically disciplined: share count reduced from 511M to 481M since FY21 (~1.2%/yr) and **52+ consecutive years of dividend increases** ($0.33 → $0.55 quarterly since 2017), payout ~60% of GAAP EPS and ~54% of FCF — comfortable.
- **But the Jetro deal is a departure.** It is a bet-the-company transaction: $29B on a $37B market cap, funded 74% with cash/debt, taking leverage to ~5.6x in a 3.7%-margin industry, requiring regulatory approval that the DOJ blocked for Sysco once before (US Foods, 2015). It also stops the buyback (only $200M repurchased in FY26 vs $1.25B in FY25) and dilutes existing holders by 19%. That is empire-building risk, even if the strategic logic — entering the higher-margin, asset-lighter cash-and-carry channel — is sound.

Capital allocation verdict: **B+ historically, unproven today.** The next 18 months will define the record.

## 5. Investment Verdict: **HOLD**

Sysco is a narrow-moat, FCF-generative, dividend-aristocrat-adjacent compounder trading at **fair value with no discount** — and carrying an unusual, self-inflicted, $29B leverage risk. GAAP earnings are *falling*, ROIC has declined 5+ points in three years, gross margins have not moved in six years, and the adjusted earnings the Street capitalizes include ~$800M of annualized costs I would not add back.

- **At $76.94:** expected total return ~7–9%/yr (2.9% dividend + 3–4% growth + modest multiple stability). That is an index-like return for above-index risk. **Hold, do not add.**
- **Would become a Buy below ~$60–62** (standalone, ~20% discount to $72) — roughly 15x normalized FCF with a 3.6%+ yield.
- **Would sell if:** the Jetro deal closes at ~5.6x leverage *and* local volume growth turns negative, or if gross margin begins compressing below 18.0% (a true moat signal).

A business at a fair price is not a buy. This is a fair business at a fair price.

## 6. Key Risks to the Value (and to a Hold thesis)

1. **Antitrust rejection of Jetro** — the single largest swing factor. Sysco lost its US Foods bid in 2015; the enforcement environment is stricter now. A blocked deal leaves dead costs and a distracted management team, likely a low-$70s stock.
2. **Leverage shock** — ~5.6x pro forma EBITDA, interest coverage near 2.8x pro forma, in a business where a 1-point margin slip is a 25% EBIT miss. A downgrade from BBB raises the cost of the $21.6B of new debt. This is the risk that most threatens permanent capital loss.
3. **Restaurant traffic deterioration** — management itself cites "continued softness in restaurant foot traffic." Independent restaurant closures shrink the customer base and force price competition.
4. **Food-cost deflation** — Q1 FY27 referenced "lower than expected U.S. food cost inflation." Sysco earns a percentage markup on case value; deflation is revenue-deflationary and margin-dilutive in absolute dollars.
5. **Recurring "one-time" costs** — $800M+/yr of transformation, restructuring and deal charges that never end. If these become permanent, normalized earnings are ~25%
