### 2. Top 3 Geopolitical Risks

**Risk 1 — China's scandium export-licence regime is an "off switch" on Bloom's growth (Probability: HIGH; Impact: HIGH–VERY HIGH)**
- Beijing added **scandium to the list requiring national-security export licences (April 2025)** — every shipment leaves at Beijing's discretion. Scandium is the core electrolyte input for every Bloom fuel cell and is also a defense-critical metal (F-35).
- Hunterbrook Media (Jul 8, 2026) traced **four China-linked supply routes** into Bloom, including direct scandium-oxide shipments to its Delaware plant and material routed through **Thailand, Japan and South Korea**; Hunan Oriental Scandium (claiming >50% of the fuel-cell-grade market) told Hunterbrook it is *"BE's largest supplier… Not exported directly."*
- Bloom's **8-K of Jul 9, 2026** (Item 7.01) "categorically rejects" the report: *"We have sufficient supply of scandium oxide to meet our current fuel cell demand and backlog, and our supply is not dependent on China,"* citing visibility for **25 GW/year** of cell production. The company's own COO blog (Jul 7, 2026) softened the earlier CEO claim of "no China supply chain" to "no *single country* can determine Bloom's destiny" — a meaningful walk-back.
- Hunterbrook's supply model implies a scandium deficit by 2028 against Wall Street's 5 GW ramp (~220t of ~240t global supply, vs. ~310t demand). Even taken skeptically, this is the single largest **un-hedgeable geopolitical dependency** in the thesis, and it sits directly on the US-China critical-minerals axis. Nearshoring options (Australia, Brazil, by-product of titanium) are years from processing scale.

**Risk 2 — US policy, permitting and tax-credit dependence (Probability: MEDIUM-HIGH; Impact: HIGH)**
- Bloom's economics rest on the **~30% federal fuel-cell investment credit** (48E), which the One Big Beautiful Bill Act preserved as a flat 30% for fuel cell property (through ~2033 per Bloom's own framing) while tightening wind/solar timelines. Any adverse Treasury guidance, repeal, or placed-in-service deadline changes hit project IRRs and therefore orders.
- **Air permitting is now a disclosed risk factor**: the Q3'26 release explicitly cites *"air permitting delays which affect the ability to finance the use of Energy Servers for customers."* **Oracle's Project Jupiter** (Doña Ana County, NM; ~7,000 Bloom fuel cells, 1.6 GW after downsizing) still lacks an approved air permit; the hearing has been repeatedly delayed and **an environmental group is suing to stop it**. Research firm SemiAnalysis has pushed first power to 2029. AEP's $2.65bn order has slipped to "no later than 2030."
- Post-midterm politics: a **Democratic Congress** raises the odds of data-center siting/emissions regulation — a **double-edged** risk (grid interconnection queues >5 years favour onsite fuel cells, but local opposition and NOx/CO₂ scrutiny of gas-fuelled cells is intensifying).
- Tariff exposure: management flags *"trade policies including tariffs"* and *"supply constraints"* in forward-looking risk language.

**Risk 3 — Korea concentration and alliance friction (Probability: MEDIUM; Impact: MEDIUM)**
- Korea is the #2 market but demand stalled: **SK ecoplant** (once ~10% shareholder, ~$566m invested) committed to volumes that propped up results, then received **three additional years to comply**, and has been **selling down its Bloom stake**. Bloom signed a Jan 2026 MoU to attack the Korean data-center market — necessary because the legacy SK channel is fading.
- Macro: US–Korea relations are under strain over trade/security; the newly negotiated **15% US tariff** band upends KORUS preferences; Fitch has flagged Korea's exposure to the US-China trade war. A weak won or Korean capital controls would raise repatriation/pricing risk, though Bloom's Korea revenue is USD-contracted.

### 3. Legal & Regulatory Highlights

- **Securities class action (active, material):** Filed in the wake of the July 8, 2026 Hunterbrook report. Class period **February 27, 2025 – July 8, 2026**; lead-plaintiff deadline **September 28, 2026** (Rosen, Kaplan Fox, Kessler Topaz, Levi & Korsinsky, Federman all soliciting). Core allegation: Bloom repeatedly told investors its supply chain was **not dependent on China, including for scandium**. Bloom's 8-K calls the claims "false and misleading" and stands behind its audited statements. Given a >$60bn market cap and a ~2,000% two-year run, a nine-figure settlement range is plausible if the case survives a motion to dismiss.
- **Derivative/shareholder demands:** Bronstein Gewirtz and others investigating derivative claims against officers/directors on the same facts — watch for D&O exposure and potential indemnification costs.
- **10-Q/A filed for Q2 2026** — an amended quarterly report, cited alongside **~73% of Q2'26 revenue from a single customer** and **43% of FY2025 revenue from a related-party (Brookfield JV) customer**. Amendments of this kind typically follow internal-control or revenue-recognition revisions and are a live audit-quality flag; expect plaintiffs to build on it and SEC staff to review.
- **Project-level environmental litigation:** the New Mexico air-permit contest over Project Jupiter (above) is litigation against a *customer's* project that directly gates Bloom revenue recognition and backlog conversion.
- **Historical precedents:** 2019 Hindenburg report and subsequent securities litigation (settled); Delaware/Delmarva tariff disputes over the legacy Bloom-backed power contracts (ratepayer costs ran >5x original projections) — a reminder that Bloom's utility/public-policy deals attract regulatory and political backlash.
- **Regulators to watch:** SEC (Division of Corporation Finance comment letters; Enforcement if a formal inquiry follows), PCAOB/auditor scrutiny of backlog vs. RPO (Bloom's $20bn unaudited backlog vs. **$442–493m of RPO** — a >40x gap vs. ~2x for a 10-peer set), NMED (New Mexico Environment Dept.), CPUC/Delmarva PSC analogues, KEPCO/Korean MOTIE.
- **No evidence found** of active DOJ/FCPA/OFAC enforcement action against Bloom.

### 4. Compliance Red Flags

1. **Export-control / critical-minerals compliance (highest priority).** A China-origin scandium chain routed through **Thailand, Japan and Korea** creates exposure to (a) China's MOFCOM licensing rules, (b) US **BIS** rules on allied-transshipment and entity-list parties, and (c) potential **disclosure-controls (Reg S-K Item 101/106)** failures. Public CEO statements of "no China supply chain" that are contradicted by trade data are the classic predicate for both SEC action and private litigation. Recommend diligence on: country-of-origin certifications, supplier audits, and whether any Chinese supplier appears on the Entity List.
2. **Related-party revenue recognition.** 74% of Q4'25 revenue ($574m of $778m) reportedly from **Brookfield JV affiliates** that Bloom's own footnotes concede *"may be a project-finance affiliate rather than the ultimate end user."* Not a sanctions issue, but a related-party-disclosure and revenue-quality issue with genuine enforcement tail risk.
3. **Government/tax-credit dependency.** Reliance on 48E credits monetised through tax-equity partners requires strict tax-equity structuring compliance; OBBBA "begin construction" and domestic-content rules create recapture risk.
4. **Data-privacy/AML:** Not material for a fuel-cell manufacturer; the only relevant angle is AI data-center customer contracts (critical-infrastructure/KYC sensitivities), which is a customer-side, not issuer-side, risk.

### 5. Overall Geopolitical/Legal Risk Rating: **HIGH**

*Rationale:* Revenue geography is benign (81% US, a low-geopolitical-risk jurisdiction), which is the mitigant. The rating is driven instead by (i) a **concentrated, China-controlled input** (scandium) that Beijing has explicitly placed under export licensing and that Bloom's management publicly — and, per trade data, inaccurately — denied depending on; (ii) an **active securities class action plus derivative investigations** built directly on that discrepancy, alongside a **10-Q/A**, a 4-CFO-in-3-years finance function and a 40x backlog-to-RPO gap; and (iii) **permitting/tax-credit policy risk** that gates the conversion of the ~$20bn backlog. Korea adds a secondary, manageable layer of country and partner risk.

**What would change the rating:** a credible non-China scandium supply chain (announced offtake with an Australian/Brazilian processor, third-party verified) → downgrade toward Medium; or an SEC formal order/restatement → upgrade toward Very High.
