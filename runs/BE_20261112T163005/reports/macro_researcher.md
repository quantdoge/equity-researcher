## 1. Macro Environment Summary — **"No-Landing Reflation with a Bear Steepener": Risk-Off for Duration, Risk-On for Credit**

The US is in an unusual and hostile configuration for long-duration growth equities as of 12 November 2026. Growth is re-accelerating (Q3 real GDP +3.4% SAAR, +2.1% YoY), unemployment has fallen back to 4.1% from 4.5% a year ago, and industrial production is +2.2% YoY — there is no recession signal anywhere in the data. But inflation has turned back up: headline CPI is **3.82% YoY** in October, versus core CPI at just **2.68%** — a 114bp gap that tells you the re-acceleration is *energy-led*, consistent with the FOMC's own April minutes citing "recent global energy price increases."

The Fed, under Chair Kevin Warsh, has reversed course. After cutting from 4.33% to 3.63% through mid-2026, it **hiked 25bp in September to an effective 3.88%**, held in October with two dissents *in favour of a hike*, and the market (Reuters poll, 10 Nov; JPM) expects another 25bp in December to 4.00–4.25%.

The critical detail is *where* the long end has gone. The 10Y has risen ~45bp since 1 June (4.44% → ~4.88–5.13%), but breakevens have **fallen** from 2.40% to 2.20%. The entire move is **real yields**: the 10Y TIPS yield went from 2.07% (1 Jun) to a 2.95% peak (5 Oct) and sits at 2.66% today. That is a term-premium/fiscal-risk repricing, not an inflation-expectations repricing — the worst possible mix, because it raises discount rates without raising the nominal growth that would justify them. The curve is a clean **bear steepener** (2Y 4.74% → 30Y 5.47%, 2s10s +39bp).

Credit, however, is calm: HY OAS at **3.30%** (from a 2.65% September trough) is historically very tight. So the macro regime is "high real rates, tight spreads, resilient growth" — a regime that punishes multiples while leaving fundamentals intact.

## 3. Company-Specific Macro Impact

Bloom is a **~$61B market cap, beta-3.64, ~60x 2026E adjusted EPS** equipment-and-power company. Its macro sensitivity is dominated by the discount rate, not by the volume line.

**Interest rates / real yields — STRONGLY NEGATIVE (dominant factor).** This is the single most important macro variable. With terminal value dominating a 60x multiple, implied equity duration is roughly 25–30 years; a 60–90bp rise in the real discount rate is mechanically worth a 20–30% multiple haircut. That is not theory — it is what happened: BE fell **26.6% in October 2026** precisely as the 10Y tagged 5.31% and real yields hit 2.95%. The stock is a levered expression of the real-rate path, and the real-rate path has turned against it. Secondary channel: **$3.0B of non-recourse project debt** (vs $2.47B recourse and $2.49B cash as of 30 Sep) funds Bloom's own/PPA generation assets. A +100bp repricing on that stack is ~$30M/yr of pretax cost — immaterial to a $61B cap, but material to *project IRRs*, which determine whether customers sign. As Bloom leans into "term power" (CEO Sridhar's framing of the $6T electricity market), it becomes structurally *more* rate-sensitive, not less.

**Growth — POSITIVE.** +3.4% SAAR GDP, +2.2% IP, and 4.1% unemployment mean Bloom's customer base (data centers, hospitals, biotech, retail, telecom) has the balance sheet and load growth to sign 10–20 year power contracts. Recession risk, the thing that would actually break the backlog, is not in the data.

**Credit conditions — NEUTRAL-TO-POSITIVE, but the key fault line.** HY OAS at 3.30% says the marginal neocloud/data-center buyer can still finance. Bloom's demand is unusually dependent on *third-party* leverage — its customers are often private, high-yield, AI-infrastructure borrowers. Tight spreads today are a green light; the 65bp widening between 1 September and 5 November is an early tremor worth watching, because if neocloud credit cracks, Bloom's order book cracks with it, independent of rates.

**Natural gas — POSITIVE.** Henry Hub at $2.63, down 21% since July, is a direct tailwind: Bloom's solid-oxide platform converts gas to electricity on-site, so cheap gas improves customer payback and widens Bloom's LCOE advantage against grid tariffs that are rising ~4% YoY. Cheap gas is the fuel that makes behind-the-meter economics work.

**Electricity prices — POSITIVE.** Retail power at ~$0.19/kWh and rising ~4% YoY, with grid interconnection queues stretching years, is the structural demand driver. Note the asymmetry: an energy-price spike *raises* Bloom's addressable economics (grid parity improves) even as it raises the Fed's hiking bias — so Bloom is a partial inflation beneficiary on the revenue line while being a victim on the discount-rate line.

**Inflation on cost structure — MILDLY NEGATIVE, offset by pricing power.** 3.8% headline CPI hits US-based manufacturing (Fremont CA, Newark DE) labour, steel, nickel and specialty alloy costs, plus tariffed imported components. But Q3 gross margin expanded and guidance was raised twice; revenue grew 138% YoY to $1.24B with FY26 guided to $4.4–4.6B. Pricing power currently dominates input inflation. Watch this if the backlog-to-revenue conversion slows.

**USD — MILDLY NEGATIVE, low materiality.** The broad dollar is +3.4% in two months on the Fed's hawkish pivot. Bloom is overwhelmingly US-revenue; the international exposure that matters is the **Bloom SK Fuel Cell JV in Korea** with SK ecoplant. A stronger dollar makes Korean won-denominated JV economics and Korean orders less attractive on translation. Not a thesis driver, but a persistent small drag if the dollar keeps firming into a December hike.

**Tariffs / trade policy — NEGATIVE, unquantified.** Fuel cell stacks and balance-of-plant include imported content; tariff escalation is a margin risk. I could not retrieve the 10-Q detail (SEC returned 403), so I am flagging this as a directional risk rather than a sized one.

## 4. Macro Risk Rating: **UNFAVOURABLE**

To be precise about *what* is unfavourable: the **operating** macro backdrop for Bloom is genuinely good — growth, gas prices, electricity scarcity, and tight credit all point the right way, and the demand drivers are structural rather than cyclical. The **financial** backdrop is bad and it is the one that sets the share price. Real 10Y yields at 2.66% (from 2.07% in June), a 30Y at 5.47%, a Fed that has flipped from cutting to hiking, and a market pricing another 25bp in December constitute a direct, quantifiable headwind to a 60x-multiple, beta-3.64 stock. October's 26.6% drawdown is the proof of transmission. Until either the term premium stops rising or earnings growth outruns multiple compression, macro is a tax on this name.

## 5. The Scenario That Would Materially Surprise: **Bear — "5.5% on the 10Y Meets a Neocloud Credit Event"**

The consensus assumes the December hike is the last and the long end stabilises. The non-consensus path: energy-led headline CPI keeps headline above 4%, the Fed is forced into a second hike in Q1 2027, the 30Y breaks 6% on term premium and fiscal supply, and — critically — the *combination* of higher-for-longer rates and a wobbling AI-infrastructure borrower pushes HY OAS from 3.30% toward 500bp. That is a double hit that BE cannot offset: multiple compression from the discount rate *plus* order deferrals as customers' project financing closes. Given beta 3.64 and a 60x multiple, a 40–50% drawdown is entirely plausible in that world, and it would look like a macro event rather than a company event.

The mirror-image bull surprise is a genuine energy shock — gas/oil spiking on geopolitics — which would make grid power scarce and expensive, force data-center operators behind the meter, and hand Bloom pricing power. Bloom's revenue would inflect upward even as the Fed tightened. Historically this company's order book has been more sensitive to the *price of power* than to the price of money. The macro risk is that in 2026–27 the price of money is winning.
