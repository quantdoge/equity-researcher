# Bloom Energy (BE) — Geopolitical & Legal Risk Assessment
*As of late November 2026. Market cap ≈ $42.1B at $142.39; beta 3.64 (source: company profile). Stock is ~50–60% off its high amid an AI-infrastructure selloff — the equity is highly headline-sensitive to exactly the geopolitical issues below.*

## 2. Top 3 Geopolitical Risks

### Risk 1 — China rare-earth (scandium) leverage and supply-chain opacity — **Probability: High | Impact: Very High**
Scandium stabilizes the ceramic electrolyte in every Bloom fuel cell. Beijing **now requires an export license for essentially every scandium shipment leaving China**, and in June 2026 tightened controls into a de facto dual-use ban on named U.S. firms (Reuters, 2026-06-22; S&P Global, 2026-06). Hunterbrook Media (July 8, 2026) reported it "traced four separate China-linked routes into Bloom's supply chain" — scandium oxide to Bloom's Delaware plant plus scandium-bearing ceramics/powders routed through **Thailand, Japan and South Korea** — citing a Hunan Oriental Scandium sales rep stating "We are also BE's largest supplier of scandium" and that material reaches U.S. customers "not exported directly." Bloom called the report's claims "false" (8-K, 2026-07-09), but there is a Chinese regulatory chokehold on a single input, layered on top of a disclosure problem, layered on top of potential **transshipment/customs exposure** in three intermediary jurisdictions. Hunterbrook also argues global scandium supply (~240t vs. ~310t demand, with Lockheed's F-35 program taking tonnage) is physically insufficient for Wall Street's 5 GW ramp — a hard constraint that no amount of capital resolves.

**Second-order consequence (underappreciated):** Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, §48E/§45Y/§45X credits are disallowed where "material assistance" from prohibited foreign entities exceeds the **material assistance cost ratio (MACR, 40% threshold beginning 2026)** (Bracewell; Plante Moran, Feb 2026; Paul Hastings). If Chinese-sourced scandium/ceramics are a meaningful share of direct material costs, Bloom's *and its customers'* tax-credit economics — a core part of the value proposition for U.S. projects — are at risk. This links the China exposure directly to U.S. project IRRs.

### Risk 2 — U.S. project-level permitting and political risk (the "domestic geopolitical" risk) — **Probability: Medium-High | Impact: High**
The flagship Oracle "Project Jupiter" site in Doña Ana County, New Mexico is the clearest example that Bloom's constraint is now *permits, not demand*. Status as of Oct–Nov 2026:
- A hearing officer refused to dismiss the air-quality permit application as administratively incomplete (Searchlight NM, Oct 27, 2026), but the state Environment Department's own counsel wrote that "material revisions at this late stage" meant the application was "not ready for primetime," after new state modeling showed **higher pollutant concentrations** than the developer's submitted modeling.
- Bloom/developers cut the fuel-cell allocation to 1,600 MW of 2,462 allocated MW and redesigned the system to cut emissions ~35% — a moving target that opponents call a due-process violation.
- **New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez is backing two state Supreme Court challenges** to Project Jupiter permits, alleging due-process failures in air permitting; the Environmental Law Center continues litigation despite a developer cease-and-desist.
- Oracle has filed a **force majeure notice**; Doña Ana County has put the project on notice for missed job-report deadlines; SemiAnalysis has pushed its base case for first power from 2027 to 2029, with cancellation a live tail risk.

Extrapolate: each gigawatt of Bloom's pipeline sits behind local air districts, county commissions, state AGs and environmental litigants. This is a structural, replicable friction — not a one-off.

### Risk 3 — South Korea market/policy dependence and the unwinding of the SK ecoplant anchor — **Probability: Medium | Impact: Medium**
Korea is Bloom's second-largest market and hosts a **consolidated joint-venture VIE** (10-Q footnotes reference a Korean JV VIE affecting receivables, inventory, PP&E and deferred profit). But the anchor is weakening: **SK ecoplant sold its entire Bloom equity stake** (Business Korea), and per Hunterbrook, after Korean demand stalled, SK ecoplant "quietly got three extra years to comply" with volume commitments. Korea's support regime (hydrogen portfolio standards, CHPS/RPS, RE100 procurement) is policy-driven and can shift with administration priorities; Korea is also expanding trade-restriction powers under its amended Foreign Trade Act (effective Oct 22, 2026) and is itself a target of a potential U.S. digital-trade probe. Net: a declining-share, policy-dependent market with a departed strategic partner — a modest but real earnings and asset-impairment risk.

**Additional watch item:** Taiwan. The MiTAC Computing partnership (islanded fuel-cell power for AI infrastructure, expanded August 2026) adds Taiwan Strait tail risk to an otherwise small revenue line.

## 3. Legal & Regulatory Highlights

**Securities class action (active, material).** Filed **July 30, 2026, N.D. Cal.**, on behalf of purchasers **Feb 27, 2025 – Jul 8, 2026**, against Bloom and certain directors/officers under **§10(b) and §20(a) and Rule 10b-5**. The core allegation: Bloom "obtained scandium through intermediaries who sourced the metal from China," thereby understating China reliance, and that statements including "*We are not dependent on China for Scandium*" and "*We are not dependent on China for a supply chain*" were materially misleading. The stock fell ~5.7% on the Hunterbrook report; the class period captures the entire 2025–26 run-up, so **damages exposure scales with a ~2,000% share-price move** — this is a nine-figure-tail case, not a nuisance suit. Lead-plaintiff deadline was Sept 28, 2026; multiple firms (Rosen, Kessler Topaz, Kaplan Fox, Zhang) are soliciting. (D&O Diary, Aug 2, 2026; firm notices.)

**Regulatory/administrative litigation.** New Mexico air permit contested case for Project Jupiter; NM AG Supreme Court challenges; county job-performance notice. Adverse rulings would directly delay revenue recognition and could trigger contractual penalties.

**Tax-credit regulatory risk.** §48E/§45Y/§45X FEOC and MACR compliance (40% threshold in 2026) — Treasury guidance (Notice 2025-08 and successors) is still being clarified; any Chinese content in direct materials is a credit-eligibility issue for Bloom's customers and JV partners.

**Trade remedies / tariffs.** Broad U.S. tariff expansion (Section 232/301 and reciprocal measures) on components and materials, plus potential retaliatory measures; Bloom has stated it "has begun changing its supply-chain process… to mitigate the impact of U.S. tariff policies" (per Hunterbrook's quote of company statements). Transshipment through Thailand/Japan/Korea invites CBP enforcement (EAPA/False Claims Act) if origin declarations are inaccurate.

**Not established / gap:** I found **no confirmed DOJ, SEC enforcement, FCPA or AML matter** against Bloom in the retrievable record, and no active patent/IP litigation. The SEC 10-K full text was not retrievable (403 on sec.gov; `fetch_filing_text` returned "No 10-K found"), so I could not audit the company's own risk-factor and "Legal Proceedings" language — that is a genuine gap in this note and should be closed before relying on it for sizing.

## 4. Compliance Red Flags

1. **Disclosure controls over supply-chain origin claims (highest severity).** Repeated public affirmations of "no China supply chain" that are now the subject of federal securities litigation, contradicted by trade data and supplier statements. Reinforces the risk of a follow-on SEC investigation and of auditor/audit-committee scrutiny of KPIs (the $20B "backlog" vs. $661.2M RPO gap is a related governance flag).
2. **Customs/transshipment and origin-declaration integrity.** Multi-country intermediary routing (Thailand, Japan, South Korea) plus an unlicensed-supplier allegation creates U.S. import-compliance exposure (CBP penalties, False Claims Act, possible criminal false-statement risk), and simultaneously **exposes Bloom to Chinese export-control law** if suppliers ship without licenses.
3. **Tax-credit compliance (FEOC/MACR).** Potential customer-facing rep-and-warranty exposure if Bloom's supply chain undermines §48E eligibility; expect indemnity claims and diligence conditions in new offtake contracts.
4. **Third-party/FCPA risk: low-moderate.** Korean JV partner relations and Asian intermediaries in a relationship-driven utility market warrant enhanced third-party due diligence, but no bribery indicators surfaced.
5. **Sanctions/Russia: no identified exposure**; low direct emerging-market exposure generally. Data privacy: not a material exposure for an industrial equipment maker.

---

## 5. Overall Geopolitical / Legal Risk Rating: **HIGH**

**Rationale.** Direct *revenue* exposure to risky jurisdictions is genuinely low — this is a ~90% U.S. business, which on a naive geographic screen looks benign. But Bloom carries a **single-point-of-failure input (scandium) controlled by a strategic adversary**, transported through three intermediary jurisdictions, on which the company made public assurances that are now the basis of a federal securities class action; and its growth engine depends on a U.S. permitting environment that has already produced a litigated, force-majeure-flagged flagship project. Add FEOC/tax-credit rules that can transfer the China exposure into customers' credit eligibility, and a Korea market whose anchor partner has exited, and the composite is High — **Very High** on the narrow question of China supply-chain/export-control exposure. The litigation tail (class period spanning a 20x share move) and the 3.64 beta make this a name where geopolitical headlines move the equity more than fundamentals do.

**What would change the rating:** credible third-party verification of non-Chinese scandium supply (Australia/Canada/Philippines sources) with disclosed volumes; MACR compliance certification from Treasury guidance; dismissal or de minimis resolution of the 10b-5 case; issuance of the Project Jupiter air permit. **What would downgrade further:** an SEC subpoena, a CBP/EAPA detainer, a permit denial or project cancellation, or evidence of revenue recognition ahead of deployment at the Brookfield-linked JVs.
