## 1. Business Quality Assessment — Moat: **NARROW**

RB Global runs two marketplaces: Ritchie Bros. (heavy equipment & transportation) and IAA (insurance salvage vehicles), plus BigIron (US agriculture, acquired May 2026).

**Sources of moat that are real:**
- **Network effects.** More bidders → higher realizations → more consignors. This is the genuine flywheel, strongest in HE&T where Ritchie Bros. is the reference price-setter globally.
- **Intangible assets / efficient scale.** Irreplaceable auction sites (~$3.1B net PP&E), long-standing insurance-carrier relationships (the largest auto relationship was just extended to all 50 states), Rouse data and SmartEquip software that raise switching costs at the margin.
- **Resilient volumes.** Q2-2026 GTV +11% to $4.67B, H1 +12% to $9.01B; FY26 GTV growth guided 9–11%, *raised* mid-year. Automotive units +11% with net share gains against Copart.

**Why not Wide — and the decisive evidence:**
- **The moat is not pricing power.** Service revenue grew only **5% against 11% GTV growth**, as the service-revenue take rate fell **110bps to 20.0%** (−140bps for H1). Management attributes this to mix (acquired businesses with lower take rates) and "automotive pricing incentives tied to higher transaction volumes" — i.e., *paying* for volume. A wide-moat network does not discount its toll.
- **Returns on total capital are inadequate.** ROIC: **22.1% (2022) → 4.6% (2023) → 7.4% (2024) → 7.1% (2025)**. The IAA deal converted a high-return business into one earning roughly its cost of capital. Pre-goodwill ROIC is ~15%, so the *operations* are fine — the *price paid* was not.
- **Growth is bought, not earned.** Excluding recent acquisitions, Q2 GTV grew 7%. Organic HE&T volumes actually *declined* ("a more cautious customer environment").

Net: a good, durable marketplace franchise whose competitive position is intact but whose economics are being shared away with customers and whose corporate returns have been structurally diluted. **Narrow, stable-to-narrowing.**

## 4. Management Quality Assessment — **C+ / B−**

- **Capital return:** dividend raised every year ($0.22 → $0.33/qtr) and, for the first time, a real buyback — 1.5M shares for $150M in Q2-2026. At ~19x earnings that is mildly accretive; at least it is not empire-building.
- **Capital allocation record:** the **IAA acquisition (~$7.3B, closed 2023) is the defining misstep.** It doubled revenue but cut ROIC from 22% to 7% and left negative tangible equity. The offset: current M&A (BigIron, agriculture) is smaller, tuck-in scale, and strategically adjacent — discipline is visibly improving.
- **Operating credibility:** guidance raised mid-year; adjusted EPS +9% in H1; cost discipline cited. CEO Jim Kessler and newer CFO Eric Guerin are executing.
- **Watch item:** a recent "leadership shake-up" was disclosed alongside the outlook. Key-person and execution risk during a turnaround of narrative.
- **Gap:** insider ownership data was not retrievable through available tools. Based on historical filings, insider ownership is low (<2%) — typical for a company of this size but not owner-operator culture.

## 5. Investment Verdict: **HOLD** (do not initiate; do not sell)

This is a genuinely understandable, network-effect marketplace with improving cash generation and a 5.6% owner-earnings yield. It is also a business earning ~7% ROIC — approximately its cost of capital — carrying 3.2x leverage against a cyclical GTV base, growing largely by acquisition, and currently watching its toll rate erode.

**A business that trades at a fair price is not a buy.** At $79.81 I estimate intrinsic value at $80–95 with a conservative point estimate near $80. That is a **0–15% discount — inadequate**. My required margin of safety implies an entry at **≤$64/share (~20% below base-case value)**, with a starter position tolerable near $68 if the take rate stabilizes.

**What would make me a buyer:** (1) service-revenue take rate flattening for two consecutive quarters; (2) net debt/EBITDA below 2.5x; (3) ROIC on total capital moving sustainably above 9–10%; (4) buybacks continuing at scale at these multiples.

**What would make me a seller:** take rate sustaining >150bps/yr of erosion, an automotive goodwill writedown, or leverage pushing above 4x.

## 6. Key Risks to the Value Thesis

1. **Take-rate compression is structural, not cyclical.** Mix shift toward lower-fee automotive/acquired channels plus "pricing incentives" means volume growth may not convert into profit growth. This is the single most important variable — it is the moat, quantified.
2. **Leverage in a cyclical business.** $4.8B net debt / ~3.2x EBITDA. A 15% GTV decline plus used-equipment price deflation would pressure both the numerator (EBITDA) and the collateral behind the assets.
3. **Goodwill impairment.** $7.1B of goodwill/intangibles (59% of assets) rests on IAA's salvage franchise holding share against Copart; negative tangible book means no asset backing.
4. **End-market softness.** HE&T transaction volumes already declining on customer caution; tariffs and construction/agriculture weakness flow straight into GTV.
5. **Competition.** Copart's superior returns in salvage; insurance-carrier concentration and total-loss frequency are outside management's control.
6. **Acquisition dependence.** Organic growth (ex-M&A) is ~7% and decelerating; each deal adds integration and multiple risk.
7. **Estimate uncertainty.** FY26E adjusted EPS and WACC inputs are my own estimates; a 100bp change in the discount rate moves fair value ~12–15%. Analyst targets of $124–145 predate the selloff and should be discounted accordingly.

**Bottom line:** Quality franchise, mediocre capital allocation history, and a share price that has finally come back to earth — but only to *fair*, not to *cheap*. Hold and wait for the price to pay you for the leverage and the take-rate uncertainty.
