## SECTOR BRIEF — Charter Communications (CHTR)
### Sector: Communication Services / US Cable & Fixed Broadband | Date: ~13 Nov 2026

---

### 1. Sector Overview

Charter competes in US cable/fixed broadband, a ~$64bn revenue market (US fixed broadband, 2025) that is now **structurally mature and shrinking in units even as it grows in dollars**. The domestic market is highly concentrated — Comcast (~$124bn), Charter (~$55bn standalone, ~$60bn+ pro forma post-Cox) and AT&T (~$126bn) control the large majority of residential broadband, giving a de facto oligopoly with an HHI well above 2,500 — yet the emergence of 5G fixed wireless access (FWA) as a genuine third pipe from Verizon, T-Mobile and AT&T has raised effective rivalry sharply without adding new fixed infrastructure. Sector revenue growth across the top-10 Communication Services names was **+6.7% in 2025** ($1,900.7bn → $2,027.1bn), but that is almost entirely Alphabet/Meta/Netflix-driven; **telecom and cable sub-segments grew 0–3%**, and the aggregated gross margin slipped from 57.6% to 54.4%. Charter's own trajectory captures the cycle: revenue growth decelerated from **+7.5% (2021) → +4.5% → +1.1% → +0.9% → −0.6% (2025)**, and pro forma (incl. Cox) Q3'26 revenue fell **1.5% YoY**. This is a late-cycle, ex-growth sector in the classic sense: penetration near saturation, pricing power eroding, capital intensity high, and returns now driven by cost extraction and balance-sheet management rather than volume.

---

### 2. Key Tailwinds

- **Consolidation-driven cost synergies and scale.** The Cox + Liberty Broadband closings (19 Aug 2026) added **11m passings and 5m customers**, and management targets **$2.0bn of incremental annualised run-rate opex savings over 18 months** — roughly 3.6% of revenue, a meaningful offset to a −1% top line. National scale also strengthens content/programming negotiating leverage.
- **Converged mobile bundling is the growth engine.** Mobile lines are growing even where broadband is not (legacy Cox added **70k mobile lines** in Q3'26 alone), lifting revenue per household and reducing churn. Charter's MVNO economics improve with volume, and wireless bundling is now the primary retention tool against FWA.
- **Deleveraging and FCF-driven re-rating potential.** Management targets **3.5x net leverage within three years**; post-Cox the equity is priced for financial distress (see §5), so any credible path to deleveraging plus a stabilising broadband base creates asymmetric optionality. BEAD's **$42.45bn** federal program also subsidises rural edge-out that cable can serve opportunistically.

### 3. Key Headwinds

- **Structural broadband subscriber erosion.** New Street's view is that **cable is unlikely to grow subscribers this decade**; FWA adds were **~3.8m in 2025** and forecast **~3.6m in 2026**, with the US FWA base potentially exceeding **20m by 2028** (Fitch). Charter and Comcast both reported broadband losses in 2025, and legacy Charter continued shedding internet subscribers in Q1'26 (−120k) with Cox losing **77k internet** in Q3'26.
- **Fiber overbuild + pricing competition compressing ARPU.** Fiber take rates averaged **~46.5% in 2024** and overbuilders plus 2027–28 BEAD-funded builds raise fixed-line overlap directly on Charter's footprint. Combined with FWA at $40–60/month, price-led competition caps ARPU growth below inflation — the mechanism by which a "growing market" still produces falling revenue.
- **Leverage and impairment risk.** Charter carries ~4.5x gross leverage; Q3'26 included a **$6.3bn impairment charge** driving a **$2.1–2.4bn net loss** (~$20.50/share). With refinancing costs well above legacy coupons (the company is tendering up to **$1.5bn** of notes), interest expense consumes a rising share of EBITDA, and the equity has become a levered call on execution.

### 5. Target Company's Competitive Position: **AVERAGE** (strong asset, weak momentum)

**Why not "strong":** Charter's asset base is genuinely best-in-class — the largest US cable footprint post-Cox (~32m customers, 41 states), sector-leading operating margins, and a fully-owned network that FWA operators must rent spectrum to match. On a pure cost-to-serve and margin basis it is the most efficient operator in the peer set. That is a durable, hard-to-replicate advantage: barriers to entry in fixed broadband remain high (capital intensity, right-of-way, decades of plant), and no rational entrant is building a third national wireline network.

**Why not "weak":** Momentum and financial flexibility are the weak link. Charter is the only major peer with contracting revenue, is losing broadband units to FWA and fiber, carries the sector's most strained balance sheet relative to its shrinking equity base, and has just taken a $6.3bn impairment. Porter analysis is unflattering: **rivalry = high** (cable vs cable, cable vs FWA, cable vs fiber); **buyer power = high** (near-zero switching costs, transparent pricing); **substitutes = high** (FWA, fiber, LEO satellite); **supplier power = moderate** (programmers, vendors, offset by scale); **entry barriers = high but bypassable via wireless spectrum**. The Cox deal buys scale and cost synergies but was struck at a moment of maximum weakness for cable equity, and integration risk is real.

Net: a structurally advantaged franchise currently earning below its cost of capital on new broadband investment, trading as a distressed credit proxy. **Average, with negative momentum** — the operational fix (cost-out + bundling) is credible but unproven, and the terminal-value debate on fixed broadband is unresolved.

---

### 6. Sector Rating: **UNATTRACTIVE**

Justification: (i) **secular unit decline** — cable is not expected to grow broadband subscribers this decade; (ii) **price/ARPU compression** from FWA and fiber overbuild means revenue growth of 0–2% at best for wireline incumbents; (iii) **rising capital intensity** (DOCSIS 4.0/fiber upgrades, BEAD-era edge-out) against a base already at ~4.5x leverage and higher refinancing costs; (iv) **cross-sector data confirms it** — aggregated Communication Services gross margin fell 330bp in 2025 even as headline revenue grew 6.7%, because growth is concentrated in asset-light internet platforms, not network operators. Within the sector, wireless-led converged operators (VZ, TMUS) are relatively better positioned than pure fixed-line cable, which is where Charter sits.

**Sector cycle position:** Late-cycle/mature, post-consolidation, with a technology transition (FWA + fiber) compressing the incumbency rent that cable has enjoyed since the 2015–2020 broadband boom. The next 2–3 years are a **share-shift and cost-extraction phase, not a growth phase**.
