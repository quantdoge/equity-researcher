## 1. Macro Environment Summary

**Stance: RISK-OFF (late-cycle tightening into a supply shock).**

The US economy is in an unusual and hostile configuration for a pre-profit, capital-intensive consumer discretionary name: **growth is holding up, but inflation is re-accelerating and the Fed has resumed hiking.** As of the Nov 18, 2026 FOMC minutes, "almost all" officials expect further increases. The Fed raised 25bp to 3.75–4.00% on Sept 16, 2026 and held on Oct 28; the effective funds rate has climbed from 3.63% (held Jan–Aug) to 3.88% in October.

The proximate cause is an **oil shock**: WTI went from $69.60/bbl (Jul 6, 2026) to $110.01 (Oct 19) — a 58% run — and, while it has since retreated to $89.53 (Nov 17), it remains ~29% above July levels. Headline CPI is 3.82% y/y (Oct 2026, index 337.476 vs 325.063 a year earlier), roughly double target, with core running ~0.3% m/m.

This has produced a **bear steepener**: the 10Y has risen 65bp since July 1 (4.48% → 5.13%), and the 2s10s spread is +39bp — positively sloped, so the curve is *not* signalling recession. It is signalling **term premium and inflation-expectation repricing**, which is a discount-rate event rather than a growth event. That distinction matters enormously for Rivian: the curve is not warning of a demand collapse, but the long end is repricing every long-duration cash flow in the market.

Underneath, the consumer is deteriorating faster than GDP suggests. University of Michigan sentiment fell to **48.1 in Sept 2026** from 71.7 in Jan 2025 (−33%), territory historically associated with recessions. 24-month personal loan rates are 11.90% (Aug 2026) vs 11.14% a year earlier. Unemployment at 4.1% (Oct) is benign and falling from 4.5% a year ago — a tight labour market that *sustains* the inflation problem and keeps the Fed hawkish.

Credit conditions are the soft spot: HY OAS has widened from 2.63% (Aug 31) to **3.52% (Nov 19)** — +89bp in eleven weeks. At 3.5% spreads are still tight in absolute terms, but the *rate of change* is a tightening signal. For a company that funds itself in the capital markets, the direction of travel matters more than the level.

The policy backdrop is a second, separate headwind: the federal EV credit (up to $7,500) was repealed effective **Sept 30, 2025**, pulling demand forward into Q4 2025 and creating a 2026 air pocket. Rivian's stock peaked at $22.64 on Dec 19, 2025 — right at that pull-forward — and has since fallen 52% to $10.93.

## 3. Company-Specific Macro Impact

**NEGATIVE — Discount rate (the dominant channel).** Rivian is a textbook long-duration asset: market cap $15.9B against ~$2.16B quarterly revenue and an 8% gross margin. Its valuation rests almost entirely on 2028–2030 R2 ramp economics discounted to today. A 10Y at 5.13% and rising, with the Fed explicitly guiding more hikes, mechanically compresses that present value. At a 1.64 beta — among the highest in Consumer Cyclical — the stock is a levered bet on the risk-free rate. This is the single largest macro headwind, and it is why a company that raised delivery guidance still trades at a 52-week low.

**NEGATIVE — Consumer auto affordability.** This is the most underappreciated channel. The $7,500 credit repeal removed a direct price subsidy, and the 10Y/5Y curve feeds straight into auto loan APRs. With 24-month personal loan rates at 11.90% and sentiment at 48.1, the marginal buyer is stretched. Critically, the **R2 (~$45k) targets a far more mainstream, rate-sensitive buyer than the R1 ($80k+)** — so Rivian's volume ramp is landing into the worst affordability backdrop in years. The affluent R1 buyer is rate-insensitive; the R2 buyer is not.

**NEGATIVE — Policy/tariff exposure.** Section 232 auto tariffs raise landed costs on imported components. Separately, Rivian's Q3 2026 gross margin of 8% was aided by **~$80M of regulatory credits** — a revenue stream that structurally shrinks as federal EV mandates are rolled back and fewer OEMs need to buy credits. That is a margin quality issue, not just a macro one: strip the credits and gross margin is closer to 4%.

**NEUTRAL-to-NEGATIVE — Rates and refinancing risk.** Genuinely mitigated. Rivian holds **$7.1B liquidity with no near-term debt maturities**, so this is *not* a near-term solvency story. The exposure is to the *cost of future capital* for the Georgia plant and R2 ramp — every 100bp on the 10Y raises the hurdle rate on capital-intensive expansion. Rising HY OAS (2.63% → 3.52%) raises the price of any future raise.

**NEUTRAL — Currency.** Predominantly US revenue, so limited translation risk. The 3.9% broad-dollar rally in ten weeks is a modest negative for component sourcing and for any international expansion, but it is a rounding error versus the rate channel.

**MIXED — Commodities.** The oil spike is a second-order negative for EV *relative* competitiveness (cheap gasoline blunts the EV value proposition), but it simultaneously improves the EV total-cost-of-ownership pitch. Net: roughly neutral on demand, negative on input costs. Battery-metal prices unavailable in this dataset — an acknowledged gap.

**POSITIVE (fragile) — Growth.** GDP at +2.1% y/y with unemployment at 4.1% means the addressable market is not contracting. A soft landing would let the R2 ramp proceed into a functioning economy. But this is the variable most at risk of flipping.

## 4. Macro Risk Rating: **UNFAVOURABLE**

Four of the five channels that matter to Rivian — discount rate, consumer affordability, policy support, and credit availability — are all moving against it simultaneously. The only genuine mitigant is the balance sheet ($7.1B liquidity, no near maturities), which converts a solvency risk into a valuation and timing risk. Note the asymmetry: GDP resilience is *bad* news for Rivian, because it keeps the Fed hiking. A strong economy here is a headwind, not a tailwind — the company needs falling rates more than it needs rising volumes.

## 5. The Scenario That Could Materially Surprise: Bear

**The Fed follows through on the minutes and takes the funds rate to 4.50%+ by mid-2027, pushing the 10Y through 5.75%.** The mechanism: oil stays near $90, core CPI refuses to break below 3%, and a resilient labour market gives the Fed no cover to pause. The 10Y breaks 5.75%, HY OAS widens through 5.00%, and the equity market re-rates long-duration growth. In that world Rivian is not a $10.93 stock — it is an $8 stock, because the R2 ramp's terminal value gets discounted at a materially higher rate at exactly the moment the company needs to raise capital for Georgia. The compounding detail is that the R2 buyer — mainstream, financing-dependent, sentiment at 48.1 — cancels orders as APRs climb, so the *volume* assumption and the *discount rate* deteriorate together. That double hit is the surprise, and the Nov 18 minutes make it the base case rather than a tail. It would show up first in the weekly R2 order/deposit data and in rising floorplan and subprime auto delinquency prints, well before it shows up in Rivian's quarterly deliveries.

**The offsetting bull case** — worth flagging for balance — is that oil's retreat from $110 to $89.53 is the first crack in the inflation story. If the shock proves transitory and the 10Y falls back to 4.00–4.25%, Rivian's discount-rate compression reverses violently and the stock re-rates toward $18–20 on an unchanged operating story. That is the payoff to the asymmetry: at 1.5x forward revenue with an 8% gross margin and a funded balance sheet, Rivian is now priced as a rate call option, not an auto company. Direction of the 10Y is the position.
