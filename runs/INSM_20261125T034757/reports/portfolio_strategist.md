## 3. Position Sizing Recommendation

### **New capital: 0.0% — Pass.**

### **Existing holders: reduce to a hard cap of 1.0% of portfolio.**

**The arithmetic that drives this.** Using the break value of ~$112 (midpoint of Short's $100–125 and Value's $95–105, marked up for the strategic-scarcity read-through from the Sanofi bid):

| Completion prob. | Expected gross return (7.2 months) | vs 26-wk T-bill (4.49% → 2.60%) |
|---|---|---|
| 92.3% (**market-implied**) | **+0.01%** | **−2.6%** |
| 95% | +1.01% | −1.6% |
| 97% | +1.74% | −0.9% |
| 99% | +2.32% | −0.3% |

**Holding INSM is dominated by T-bills at every plausible completion probability short of ~99%.** The market-implied 92.3% is itself roughly the *correct* base rate for a committed-financing, no-financing-condition, low-overlap strategic deal — so there is no mispricing to harvest, in either direction.

**Risk-budget check at a 3% weight** (the size a "high-conviction biotech" would normally carry):
- Spread capture: 2.85% × 3% = **+8.5bp** of portfolio
- Break hit: 7.7% × −34% × 3% = **−79bp** of portfolio
- Net expected: **≈ −0bp**, with a fat left tail and 7 months of illiquidity

You are running a 79bp downside risk to earn 8.5bp. That is a 1:9 risk/reward on a near-zero expected value — indefensible in a return-seeking sleeve.

**Kelly check:** f* = (p·b − q)/b where b = 2.85%, q = 7.7% → f* is **negative**. Kelly says size zero. The only legitimate positive size is a *topping-bid option* (see §6), which caps the position at 1.0%.

**Why 1.0% and not 0% for existing holders:** transaction costs, index-fund constraints where selling forces an off-benchmark rebalancing cost, and the ~5–8% probability of a $185–195 topping bid (the $1.125B break fee deters but does not eliminate it; the board retains a fiduciary out). Beyond 1.0%, the position is an unintended concentration with no compensating return.

**Critically: the tax-deferral argument for holding is weak.** The consideration is **all cash**, so the position is a taxable event in H1-2027 regardless. Selling now defers nothing but ~7 months of tax timing. That removes the single strongest conventional reason to sit in a thin arb.

## 4. Portfolio Fit Analysis

**Sector:** Healthcare/biotech line item becomes **misleading**. Post-deal, INSM is a short-duration USD cash claim with Sanofi counterparty risk. Risk reporting must **reclassify it out of biotech growth exposure into the event/cash sleeve**, or the fund will believe it holds biotech beta it has already sold.

**Factor exposure — the key insight:** INSM's pre-deal R² vs SPY was **0.006** and correlation to XBI was **0.386** (Quant). It never provided biotech factor exposure — it was pure idiosyncratic jump risk that *diversified nothing* and *hedged nothing*. Post-deal, that jumps to a binary completion risk **correlated with nothing in the book** — which looks diversifying on a correlation matrix but is a fat left tail. Do not let a low-correlation number justify the position.

**Concentration / look-through:** Owning INSM is economically **short Sanofi credit + long deal completion**. If the fund holds other large-cap pharma M&A paper, this stacks deal-break risk. If the fund holds SNY equity, the position is a partial offset (fine, but not a reason to own it).

**Rate / duration:** The claim's duration collapses from ~15 years (pre-deal biotech multiple) to **~0.6 years**, at a 5.13% 10Y / 4.37% front end. There is genuine opportunity cost: at a 10% equity hurdle, 7 months of capital at 4.7% annualised forgoes **~310bp** of return, ≈1.8% on the position's capital.

**Liquidity:** Not a constraint (2–3M shares/day normal; 79M on the event day), so exit is executable without market impact — which removes the usual "we're trapped" defence for holding.

## 7. Final Portfolio Action: **PASS (new capital) / REDUCE to ≤1.0% (existing)**

**Rationale in one line:** The Sanofi deal is more likely than not to close (~92%), but at $170.15 the residual 2.85% spread **does not pay for a −34% tail**, leaving an expected return of approximately zero over 7 months against 2.6% risk-free — a position that is Dominated by cash, misclassified as biotech exposure, and carries a documented process-integrity conflict (ex-Sanofi CEO on the board during the sale; an officer sale during live rumours).

**What would change my mind — and I want to be explicit, because the specialists are split:**
- A **topping bid** at $185+ transforms the payoff and justifies holding to terms.
- A spread blow-out to **8–12% ($155–162)** on a *resolvable* scare (litigation noise, not a second request) makes this a genuine, well-paid arb.
- Disclosure of a **large reciprocal Sanofi reverse termination fee** would meaningfully improve the risk allocation and would justify up to 2%.

Absent those, the honest answer to "should we own INSM?" is: **the business earned the bid; the shareholders have been paid; there is no return left to collect.**
